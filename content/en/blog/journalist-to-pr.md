Journalists and PR professionals use the same language but look in opposite directions.

A journalist asks "What is true?" while PR asks "How do we communicate this?" That difference was uncomfortable at first.

**What Prompted the Switch**

Twelve years on the beat meant meeting a lot of corporate PR people. The good ones were different. They understood the journalist's question and offered what the journalist needed before being asked. They weren't simply delivering a message — they were speaking the journalist's language.

That was only possible for people who genuinely understood journalism. Most PR people were defensive, in a guarded posture by default — but because both sides need each other, there were moments of real alignment and moments of subtle friction.

**What I Discovered After the Move**

The strength of a journalist-turned-PR is empathy — the ability to see from the other side. I can judge "will this press release become an article?" from a journalist's perspective. I know which angles become stories, which figures are persuasive, which phrases will be quoted.

I can also gauge what's needed at a given moment, what kind of pitch makes sense right now. When a journalist calls something urgent, I have a reasonable read on whether it actually is. And I develop a sense for what can't be disclosed — that instinct builds in.

There are weaknesses too. Journalism is fundamentally solo work; PR is fundamentally about coordinating stakeholders. Internal approval processes, executive communication, team management — these require different muscles than reporting. Media matters, but it can't be the only thing you're managing. There's a kind of comprehensive judgment required — and then the decisiveness to act boldly when needed.

**What's the Same in the End**

Whether journalist or PR, looking back, the core is identical: accurately understanding a complex reality and translating it into language the other person can actually receive.

The tools and directions differ. That's all.

We still joke about who's the client and who's the vendor, but the work of persuading, earning empathy, and moving people — that's the same on both sides.

Loving what you do is hard. Understanding it — that becomes possible the moment you step back slightly and look at the situation around you.

Don't be swayed by every small win or setback. Think deeply, then act. That's what the moment calls for.
