The Power of True Story: A Review of the Film Rebound


**1. The Power of True Story — The Appeal of Sports Films**

The real story appears near the end of the film, but to explain it briefly: **what happened at the 2012 high school basketball championship was genuinely like something out of a manga.** A basketball team from Busan Middle School with only six registered players caused a sensation at the tournament. Against the nation's top teams, with no substitutions available, they pulled off something close to a miracle. In a tournament with games packed back to back every day, the improbable became real.

As director Jang Hang-jun said in interviews:

> "I couldn't resist."

For sports fans especially, stories of underdogs pulling off miracles against astronomical odds are the kind you want to watch again and again. **Particularly if you're a Slam Dunk generation fan whose heart was already warmed by the recently released "The First Slam Dunk" — you won't want to miss this film.** It also works perfectly as a movie for couples or families. Sweat-soaked sports stories almost always deliver more emotion than you expect.

I'd love to walk through the full story scene by scene, but that would be spoiling things — so I'll hold back.

The film grips onto reality and doesn't try to beautify it. Instead, it lets the facts do the work of making the story shine.

The character names in the film are those of the real people involved, and even the referees are actual officials from the Korean Basketball Association. Director Jang reportedly held auditions for around 500 actors before casting, and that dedication shows in the film's quality. **There's no shoehorned romance or teen drama forced in to make the high school setting feel authentic, and the backstory of Coach Kang (played in the film as Kang Yang-hyun) is never dragged out to manufacture cheap sympathy.**

Sports are defined by clear outcomes — wins and losses — and the process is easily forgotten once the game ends. Focusing on restoring and recreating that process for audiences is the director's gift, and proof that he understands the charm of sports films better than most.

This sense of realism likely stems from years of preparation. The cast members cast as players are genuinely tall — at the level of real basketball players — and all have outstanding athletic ability. **Ahn Jae-hong, who plays Coach Kang, stands 178 cm** — taller than his round, approachable face might suggest. He's actually from Busan, so his regional dialect sounds completely natural. By all accounts, the real Coach Kang himself said the actor felt like the genuine article.

**Lee Shin-young, who plays point guard Kibum — a former middle school MVP fallen into a slump — is also from Gyeongsang province and stands 180 cm.** And **Jeong Jin-woon (playing Gyuhyeok), formerly of 2AM, is one of the entertainment industry's most passionate basketball fans**. He had actually dreamed of becoming a professional player and had multiple ankle surgeries before letting the dream go — making him perfect for the role. Both actors are recognizable idol figures, but the film wisely resists over-focusing on their rivalry or personal arc.

**In Slam Dunk terms, they'd be the Seo Tae-woong and Kang Baek-ho positions** — but unlike that iconic duo's playful chemistry, Kibum and Gyuhyeok carry real, deep-seated animosity. Still, the bathroom conversation scene where they quietly show concern for each other leads to a high-five moment that inevitably evokes Slam Dunk's legendary high-five scene — a small but satisfying touch.

The other players also click perfectly into their characters even without heavy screen time. Sungyu, played by Kim Taek, stands 192 cm and is a physical education graduate from Whimun High School and Chung-Ang University. Having one actual athlete in the cast immediately elevates the film's credibility. Kangho, played by Jeong Geon-joo, also stands 187 cm. The role of **Michael Jordan-fanatic Jinwook (Ahn Chi-ho) has a brief but impactful presence that somehow evokes both Kang Baek-ho and Jeon Ho-jang — good for a knowing smile**.

Finally, Jaeyun — the sixth man who plays a crucial role in the final stretch — is played by Kim Min, and he delivers. He stands 180 cm, which was itself a surprise. In a six-player team, Jaeyun is the only substitute, and his role is a perfect embodiment of the sporting maxim that effort never betrays you. **He brings to mind Slam Dunk's own embodiment of effort, Haenam's Hong Ikhyun** — an early-stage version, before the relentless training that would eventually make Hong a three-point specialist despite his modest frame. He also carries a bit of **the glasses-wearing sixth man energy** — quietly lulling the opposing bench into lowering their guard before delivering a crucial blow.

**2. Memorable Scenes and Lines**

A few standout moments and thoughts:

**The Right Kind of Humor**

This is Director Jang's signature strength. He finds small laugh moments without disrupting the tone. The scenes featuring the school teachers are a perfect example — their reaction to the team's journey probably mirrors how many middle-aged fans in the audience feel too. Ahn Jae-hong's face is ideal for delivering this everyday humor.

> "Well, let's give it a go — just to fill out the roster."

Despite what the words suggest, Coach Kang's sincerity is unmistakable. People who play sports always give their whole selves.

**The Meaning of "Rebound"**

When you hear "rebound," the first thing that comes to mind is probably "the man who controls the boards controls the game" — that classic Slam Dunk line.

A defensive rebound resets the team and launches the offense. An offensive rebound creates one more chance. Compared to the glamour of guards and shooters, rebounding often goes underappreciated. But Slam Dunk made me think again about what a rebound really means — and this film adds one more layer. **If you truly want it badly enough, how fiercely you jump and commit determines whether you get another chance. And that's true in life too.** Before saying "I gave it my best, there was nothing more I could do" — did you crash the boards, fight for position, and think about grabbing that second chance?

> What can turn failure into success — the rebound.

**When You Can Play Free of Pressure, That's When It's Beautiful**

Obviously, pushing through seven games with only six players is impossible. Our bodies need recovery time, and mental strength has its limits. Buksan losing after beating Sangwang High School and falling in the very next game has a narrative logic to it — but it's also realistic. Still: never give up. After the second loss, Coach Kang keeps telling his players to enjoy it. Once the burden is lifted, the kids go on to create miracles.

Personally, **what I loved most was the gestures and playful signals the players would throw toward the coach each time they sparked a comeback.** We're so accustomed to thinking that sports are about competition, that losing means nothing was gained — that even high school basketball comes with its share of coaches who rule through fear and criticism. But that kind of coaching might squeeze 110% out of a player. It won't get you to 120% or 130%.

Kids who love basketball and find genuine joy in their own plays, and a coach who receives that joy — that's what real education looks like. **In that spirit, I prefer Klopp to Ferguson.**

> Let's enjoy today — without regret, without anything left undone.

**3. Higher Quality Than Expected**

Good ingredients make for good food — that's obvious. But taking those ingredients and cooking them well, then plating them beautifully, is a different skill entirely.

Director Jang Hang-jun — who has mastered the art of blending cinematic sensibility with mainstream appeal through variety shows and dramas — played his trump card here. **Screenwriters Kwon Seong-hwi (of *Narcos* fame) and Kim Eun-hee (Jang's wife and the writer behind *Signal* and *Kingdom*) co-wrote the script**, turning what could have been a predictable heartwarming sports movie into something more lean and interesting. (Kim Eun-hee's scripts are, personally, among my favorites.)

**The decision to keep the focus on the basketball games and tournament — the film's core — rather than on the characters' personal backstories, stands out.** Dramas and variety shows typically build up characters to build emotion: the lonelier the character, the more desperate the love; the harder the circumstances, the greater the sympathy. Emotional amplification tends to be character-driven rather than story-driven.

But this film briskly lays down the character foundations in the first half, then commits to the eight-day high school basketball tournament for the second half. Honestly, the early portion — where Coach Kang joins Jungang High School and struggles through his first tournament amid his own stubbornness and failures — felt a little slow at times. But **just as the film's title suggests, the moment of the shot is brief, and the "rebound" that follows is ferocious**.

The second half, covering qualifying matches through the final, makes excellent use of long takes to draw out everything that makes a sports film great. Basketball is already a sport whose court-level intensity translates well to spectators — in a cinema, with direction added, it comes alive even more vividly. **Rather than the polished glamour of the NBA, what you get is the sweaty realism of a high school court, with just the right level of nail-biting play — not overdone, not underdone.** The live commentary from actual broadcasters amplifies this effect further.

As a side note: Cheon Gi-beom (the real player the lead character is based on) won four individual awards at the actual tournament — MVP, top scorer, best defender, and best assist player. The film leaves this out. There's a saying about not putting too much food on a single plate — this is a good example of following that principle.

**On a lighter note, the color palette of the film — very much in the teen movie vein — won me over.** The skies and sea of Busan look genuinely beautiful, almost impossibly so. A slightly pastel color grade gives the footage a translucent quality that somehow evokes the clarity and brightness of high school days.

**4. Why the Game Company Nexon?**

The first thing that caught my eye when the film started was Nexon. **Professional habit, perhaps — my first thought was: Nexon is funding a film? This was their first investment in a commercial film.**

Nexon's homepage had a full Rebound pop-up. That was a surprise.

When the production team was on the verge of giving up after losing investors, Nexon CEO Lee Jeong-heon stepped in with funding. The production budget was reportedly around 10 billion won — not a simple decision. The break-even point was 18 billion won. Lee said: "We participated in production to convey enjoyment and emotion beyond gaming in other areas."

My read on Nexon's thinking: they saw the storytelling potential and scored it highly. Nexon is known for focusing on the fundamentals of what they do — and beyond tech, they understand that "story" is the foundation of games, and by extension, all entertainment. CEO Lee has long emphasized IP (intellectual property), noting that original storytelling is the source material from which webtoons, novels, films, and games all spring. The January of the previous year saw Nexon invest 600 billion won to acquire AGBO — the studio behind *Captain America: The Winter Soldier* and *Avengers: Endgame*, run by the Russo Brothers — which also reflects this belief in the fusion of film and games, rooted in storytelling.

Nexon is currently co-building an immersive digital media center in Sejong City with Naver, YG Entertainment, WizeWorks Studio, and NP — and I'm watching with interest to see what emerges from that collaboration.

**5. Is the Basketball Film Era Upon Us?**

**The basketball film wave kicked off by Slam Dunk shows no signs of letting up.** As I noted in a previous post, basketball's golden age in Korea was the 1990s — defined by university powerhouses like Yonsei and Korea University, and by stars like Seo Jang-hoon and Hyun Joo-yeop, who are now better known as TV personalities. The NBA-equivalent of that era, the Basketball Festival (농구대잔치), featured star players like Heo Jae, Kang Dong-hee, and Moon Kyung-eun at clubs like Kia, Samsung, and Hyundai.

But nostalgia gets gilded with time — and that gilding is worth money. Slam Dunk merchandise is selling again, and collector's editions are sold out. Marketing always gravitates toward what resonates with the public, which could signal that basketball's moment may be returning. **My wife says basketball is her favorite sport, because: 1) games are short, 2) the court-level intensity is electric, and 3) players are tall and good-looking.**

These are excellent selling points for a mainstream audience. Stories like Heo Jae's son Heo Hoon — featured in the film — are exactly the kind of narrative that could fuel a KBL revival. (He's an MVP-caliber player, and by all accounts, very good-looking.)

The film *Rebound* also cleverly cast actors who closely resemble the real players in the closing sequence, showing actual photos. **This could translate directly into renewed interest in real basketball.** Even the actual players spent months advising the film crew on basketball. Fans have already started comparing the real people to the fictional characters online — with predictably heated commentary.

Add to this the recent release of *AIR* — the story of Michael Jordan and Nike's Air Jordan line, with a perfect 100% on Rotten Tomatoes — and interest in basketball looks set to continue for some time.

To wrap up: spring means plenty of reasons to be outdoors rather than at the cinema. But if you do find yourself looking for a film to see, *Rebound* won't leave you with regrets.

**The rim is round, and even when the game ends, life goes on.**
